Overthinking Boundary in Reasoning LLMs
Master's Thesis Proposal for 625.803–804
Student: Aditya Bhatt (Applied & Computational Mathematics)
Research Adviser: Dr. Zerotti Woods
Second Reader: Dr. Moustapha Pemy
1. Problem Motivation
Reasoning-oriented large language models often benefit from intermediate steps, but the marginal value of
further reasoning is not uniformly positive. Beyond a model-dependent and task-dependent point, additional
inference can revise a correct answer, drift away from the relevant solution path, or consume compute without
improving correctness. This thesis studies that transition point, called the overthinking boundary, and asks
whether it can be defined mathematically, detected empirically, and used as a practical stopping rule.
This topic fits applied and computational mathematics because it combines stochastic modeling, sequential
decision-making, hazard processes, uncertainty quantification, and empirical analysis of computational
systems. The phenomenon is analogous to batch overtraining in supervised learning and to overthinking an
exam question and changing a correct answer to a wrong one.
2. Research Question and Contribution
The main research question is whether the point at which further reasoning becomes counterproductive
in a reasoning language model can be characterized as a mathematically meaningful stopping boundary
and estimated from observable trace features.
Recent literature on adaptive test-time compute suggests that longer chain-of-thought is not uniformly
beneficial, but much of that work relies on proxy scores or heuristics rather than an explicit continuation-value
formulation. The intended contribution is to formalize overthinking as an optimal stopping problem with
distinct repair and corruption hazards and to test that framework on real traces from open-weight reasoning
models. Unlike a pure confidence or entropy heuristic, the framework separates beneficial continuation from
harmful continuation and places the problem at the intersection of stochastic processes, sequential inference,
and modern AI systems. The thesis therefore aims to formalize a continuation-value framework, study whether
its drift admits an operational stopping boundary, estimate the latent quantities from observable traces, and
compare the resulting rules against fixed-length, never-stop, and sequential-detection baselines.
3. Proposed Methodology
Analytical formalization. To detect the overthinking boundary, this study treats each reasoning trace as a
jump process with competing repair and corruption hazards. Let τ be a stopping time with respect to the
observable filtration Ft. For a fixed problem instance, let G denote the deterministic ground-truth answer, let
At denote the candidate answer produced after step t, and define correctness by Ct = I(At = G). Even though G
is fixed, At and Ct are random because the model's reasoning trajectory is stochastic. The randomness enters
through the sampled reasoning path and the stopping time, not through the truth itself.

CORE OBJECTS
G
fixed ground-truth answer
At
candidate answer after t steps
Ct
correctness indicator, equal to 1 when At = G
qt
conditional probability of correctness
OBJECTIVE
E[Cτ - λτ | F0]
The expectation is over repeated sampled reasoning paths, together with the induced stopping time, not over
the objective truth of the answer. Conditioning on the observed trace gives the current correctness belief qt =
P(Ct = 1 | Ft) = E[Ct | Ft]. Let St = qt - λt denote the expected utility of stopping immediately at step t. The
Bellman recursion then compares stopping now against continuing one more step.
BELLMAN RECURSION
Ut = max{St, E[Ut+1 | Ft]}
This recursion is distinct from the one-step continuation gain. The latter is the marginal value of one more
reasoning step rather than the recursion itself.
ONE-STEP CONTINUATION GAIN
μt = E[St+1 - St | Ft]
= (1 - qt) αt - qt βt - λ
Here αt = P(Ct+1 = 1 | Ct = 0, Ft) is the repair hazard, βt = P(Ct+1 = 0 | Ct = 1, Ft) is the corruption hazard, and
λ is the per-step compute cost. In plain terms, αt is the chance that one more step fixes a currently wrong
answer, while βt is the chance that one more step breaks a currently correct answer. Under a one-crossing
latent model, the canonical boundary is the first step at which μt becomes nonpositive. In empirical traces,
nonmonotone drift can require distinguishing an early warning crossing from the last usable positive window.
If qt rises early while αt falls and βt rises, then μt should plausibly cross zero at most once, yielding the
candidate boundary
CANDIDATE BOUNDARY
T* = inf{t : μt ≤ 0}
Equivalently, μt > 0 defines a continuation region in which another step has positive expected value, whereas
μt ≤ 0 marks the stopping region.
TOY TRACE INTERPRETATION
Suppose a model starts an arithmetic problem with a wrong answer, repairs it, and later changes that correct
answer after excessive reflection. Early on, repair can dominate corruption, so μt > 0 and continuation helps;
later, corruption can dominate, so μt ≤ 0 and stopping is preferable. This matches early stopping in training
and exam-answer overthinking.

Estimation and experiment. The hidden variable in the problem is correctness during the trace: at inference
time, the model does not reveal whether its current answer is actually correct. The thesis will therefore
estimate qt, αt, and βt from observable trace features including answer revisions, entropy summaries, trace
length, and hidden-state proxies where available. These quantities will be estimated in two layers: a
correctness model for qt and transition models for repair and corruption at the next step. Because the stop rule
scans the trace repeatedly, the thesis will compare pointwise concentration methods with anytime-valid
procedures such as empirical-Bernstein bounds and e-process-style detectors.
Offline, benchmark problems with known answers allow traces to be labeled for training and evaluation.
Online, the rule sees only observable trace features, so it must act on predicted values of qt, αt, and βt rather
than on ground-truth correctness itself.
The empirical study will use real traces from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Mistral-7B-Instruct on GSM8K-style exact-answer problems. The main
comparisons will be among hazard-based drift rules, statistically valid sequential detectors, fixed-length
policies, and never-stop baselines, with evaluation by answer accuracy at stop time, average stop step,
compute-adjusted utility, oracle regret, and robustness across tasks and model families.
4. Expected Results and Significance
The expected outcome is a mathematically rigorous and empirically validated framework showing that
overthinking can be analyzed as a stopping phenomenon. The thesis does not require one universal stop step
shared by every model family; rather, it aims to identify a common hazard-based framework whose boundary
location depends on the balance between repair and corruption. Theoretically, this formalizes the optimal-
compute problem for autoregressive reasoning; practically, it enables higher answer accuracy and lower
inference latency in resource-constrained environments.
An important distinction is between the latent boundary T* defined using idealized correctness quantities and
the operational stopping rule τ̂, which must be computed from observable trace features alone. A successful
outcome is therefore not merely defining the ideal boundary, but showing that a data-driven rule can track it
closely enough to achieve low regret relative to the oracle boundary while saving test-time compute.
5. Preliminary Work and Semester Plan
Substantial preliminary work has already been completed, including the mathematical framework, a full trace-
generation and evaluation pipeline, and instrumented runs on DeepSeek, Qwen, and Mistral families on
GSM8K-style tasks. Current evidence suggests that the boundary is capability-dependent: stronger families
can sustain longer repair-dominant windows before corruption dominates. Phase 1 (625.803) will finalize the
stochastic formulation, complete the literature survey, and consolidate the cross-family evaluation pipeline.
Phase 2 (625.804) will run larger empirical studies, evaluate robustness across difficulty strata and model
families, and complete the final thesis and defense materials. This research will be conducted under the
guidance of Dr. Woods and Dr. Pemy and prepared in accordance with the Johns Hopkins University Master's
thesis guidelines.
